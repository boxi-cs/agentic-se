English is the first teaching language. By the end of the course you can deliver changes in a real codebase with a coding agent, confirm that the agent got them right, and explain and modify your own work without an agent.

The course follows the public Fall 2026 calendar of Stanford CS146S in its topics, their order and the position of its deadlines, and adds three things: two weeks on verification (weeks 7 and 10), a project defense, and one decision line at the end of every assignment (accept, narrow, recheck or stop) with the evidence behind it. That course's assignment repository carries no licence, so only its public structure is followed: every assignment, lecture note and rubric here is written for this course, and no text is copied.

Course pages, lecture materials and rubrics are being rearranged to this calendar. Where another page still describes the earlier fourteen-week layout, this syllabus takes precedence.

Overview

  • Audience and prerequisites: senior undergraduates and taught master's students in computing or software engineering, with programming, data structures, Git and testing. No experience with AI coding tools is assumed.
  • Length: 12 teaching weeks, then an assessment week (expo and defenses) in week 13.
  • Two sessions a week: the first is a lecture that generates and then verifies, live. The second is a practitioner guest; in a week without a guest it is a studio, where you work on your own project or contribution while staff circulate. Each session is 90 minutes; the plan is one cohort of 60 students.
  • Three lines of work: six assignments (mostly in the first six weeks), four open-source contributions (across two repositories), and one term project (one or two people).

How the course is designed

  1. Verification first: every example shows both the generating step and the verifying step. No lecture shows "let the agent finish it".
  2. Individual AI-off evidence is a pass condition: two in-class checkpoints and the defense carry 30 marks; a student below half of them, or below 5 of the 15 defense marks, does not pass.
  3. Marked on engineering evidence: the project and the contributions are marked on the specification, instruction file, gates, tests, evaluation and your own verification, not on how the interface looks or how many features there are.
  4. Contributions do not depend on luck: a mark never depends on whether a maintainer replies; where a repository has no partner contact, a teaching assistant gives an acceptance review.

Learning outcomes

  • LO1: Explain a coding agent's loop, system prompt and tool definitions, and predict how it fails on context, tools and stopping.
  • LO2: Design an agent development workflow: context and specifications, MCP tools, skills, instruction files, hooks, subagents and background tasks.
  • LO3: Write a requirement as a specification an agent can implement and a test can check, and revise it when new evidence arrives.
  • LO4: Make an unfamiliar real codebase agent-ready, and deliver a change in it without breaking callers.
  • LO5: Verify agent-written code with regression and mutation tests, code review, static analysis and security scanning, and defend each acceptance.
  • LO6: Evaluate agents: check that a test can fail, that the denominator is complete and that a judge is reliable, and read small-sample results correctly.
  • LO7: Recognise and mitigate agent security risks (prompt injection, credentials, supply chain), verifying a defense with a local reproduction and a regression.
  • LO8: Describe how a team operates agents (portals, gateways, routing, cost), and how to recover from an incident and prove the recovery with a regression.
  • LO9: Collaborate responsibly: follow upstream rules, disclose AI use honestly, submit no low-effort generated work, and decide again when the inputs change.

Passing requires at least 60/100 overall, at least 15/30 across CP1, CP2 and the defense, and at least 5/15 on the defense. There are no bonus marks. An assessment not yet taken stays missing; it is not scored as zero, and the course is not passed while a checkpoint or the defense is missing.

For each of CP1, CP2 and the defense, a student who first scores below half may take one equivalent resit with the same learning outcomes, duration and difficulty after feedback. The effective mark is the higher of the two, and a resit can replace the original with at most half of the item's maximum. Both the original record and the resit record are kept. A sitting that was validly passed cannot be retaken to raise the mark. A sitting voided by a technical failure or an approved accommodation is replaced by an equivalent sitting and does not use up the resit.

Assignments

Six assignments are worth 2.5 marks each. Each one hands in the work, the verification result and a short explanation, and ends with one decision line and the evidence behind it. Note your AI use once. Assignments are marked on completion and evidence; there are no hidden tests.

Marks go to engineering evidence only. Any topic is allowed, but a project with an interface and features and no specification, gate, tests or evaluation earns no milestone marks. The two members of a pair share the marks for the proposal, specification, alpha and final; the defense is marked individually. The expo is held in week 13 before the defenses and counts towards participation.

  • Small, then substantial: the first contribution in each repository is a small onboarding fix (5 marks); the second is a substantive change (10 marks).
  • Two repositories: you rank your preferences in week 2 and the teaching team assigns your first repository; after contribution 2 you move to a second one. The point is to onboard onto two different real codebases.
  • Project list: public open-source projects that partner companies maintain or depend on come first, with other active projects added. A project qualifies with an OSI licence, contribution guidelines, an AI-use policy that permits the work, and no CLA that assigns copyright. At most five students work on one repository at a time. You do not make a marked contribution to a repository owned by your own residency employer. The number of partner repositories and maintainer contacts is not yet confirmed.
  • Completion: in a repository with a partner contact, a merged pull request meets the completion condition. Where there is no contact, or a maintainer has not replied within seven days, a teaching assistant gives an acceptance review against the project's own contribution guidelines instead.
  • Quality line: low-effort AI output scores zero; the maintainer or the teaching assistant decides. Each contribution needs a check that fails before the change and passes after it, and your own answers to what changed, why, which inputs could break it, and what you verified yourself.
  • Boundaries and rights: contribute to public repositories only. Residency, employer or partner-internal code never enters the course or a model. You may use a pseudonym where the project permits it. If you will not publish, or will not sign a CLA or DCO, you may take the course-hosted equivalent route without giving a reason, with the same rubric and maximum.

Workload and participation

There is no hour budget. Weekly time estimates are published after a timed human pilot and include out-of-class cost honestly; required content is not cut to fit a total.

The second session each week is offered to a practitioner for at most 45 minutes, invited for that week's topic, with no product pitch and with sources for any numbers. Write one question beforehand. The course runs with however many guests can come; the number is not yet confirmed. Participation marks cover guest questions, studio work and the expo.

Privacy, fairness and accessibility

Course repositories, prompts, session records and marking material carry no real credentials and no unrelated personal data. Submit only the smallest process record that shows your work, redacted first. Required materials and activities have keyboard-operable, captioned or text alternatives, and you may ask for equivalent time or an equivalent way of taking part; an accommodation keeps the same learning outcomes and the same attainable marks. Oral defenses use a shared rubric and comparable answering time, and a second calibrated marker reviews cases near a threshold, cases where markers disagree and cases a student asks to have reviewed. Course data is not used for teaching research; any later use needs ethics approval and your separate consent first.

Tools and model access

  • Coding agent: you use a mature coding agent directly. The course states the capabilities it needs (instruction files, hooks, a permission mode, MCP, subagents, a readable session record) and names no vendor.
  • Seats and credit: every student has a working agent seat or credit when teaching starts, at no cost to the student. Teaching credit from partners or providers is used first, and the course budget buys the rest; how much partners provide is not yet confirmed.
  • Safety: run the agent in a dedicated directory or container; keep residency, employer and cloud credentials out of that environment; keep keys out of repositories, prompts, screenshots and logs; run the injection demonstration offline against the local machine only. The Week 0 setup page explains each practice and how to check it.
  • Other tools: pytest, one mutation-testing tool and one property-testing library, Semgrep, gitleaks, pip-audit.

AI use and responsibility

The checkpoints and the defense are done alone. All other work may use an agent, noted once in the work. Authorship is never inferred from writing style; contradictory evidence goes to a human process.
