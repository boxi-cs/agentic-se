English is the first teaching language. Over fourteen weeks, you will learn to enter an unfamiliar repository, clarify a user need, delegate a bounded task to an agent, verify the result and explain the decision yourself.

The teacher uses a continuing CI dashboard case. Each student enters their own copy of the supplied ci-dashboard repository in week 3 and uses it alone to practise testing, security, evaluation, incident recovery and audit verification on one path. You build a complete feature in a real open-source project instead, through two or three approved contributions. Optional features and advanced tool integrations are not required for the highest rubric band.

Learning outcomes

  • LO1: Explain model and agent behavior, including context, tools and stopping failures.
  • LO2: Design a bounded agent workflow with relevant context, permissions and resource awareness.
  • LO3: Turn user discovery into executable requirements and acceptance criteria.
  • LO4: Understand and improve an existing repository while preserving required behavior.
  • LO5: Verify generated code and independently explain tests, reviews and merge decisions.
  • LO6: Design and audit evaluations, interpret traces and calibrate evaluators.
  • LO7: Reproduce and mitigate local agent and application security failures.
  • LO8: Deploy, observe and recover a product and explain incident decisions.
  • LO9: Work responsibly, attribute assistance and calibrate claims against evidence.

Passing requires at least 60/100 overall and 20/40 across the two independent checks and oral defense. Missing components remain missing until resolved; they are not treated as zero or passing. There are no bonus points.

P1 (agent explanation), P2 (specification-constrained review) and P3 (evaluation audit) are the only separate personal practice submissions, worth 4, 3 and 3 points. Keep the artifact, verification, your own short explanation and feedback-based revision. Repository context and discovery feed M0, testing and security feed M1, and the evaluator workshop feeds M2. In the repository-context workshop each student also writes one standard skill for the tracing method, checks when it triggers, uses it once on ci-dashboard and once read-only on your candidate contribution project, and revises and tests a small MCP tool server. Weekly self-checks replace a separate quiz system; there is no CP3 or extra reflection report.

The oral defense uses four integrated domains: requirements and code paths, verification and evaluation, security and incident recovery, and a changed-input responsibility decision. LO3, LO6, LO7 and LO8 are required; the defense transfers rather than repeats every outcome one by one. The project demonstration cannot replace it.

Feedback and reassessment

Each independent component below 50% is eligible for one equivalent resit after feedback, covering the same outcomes, duration and difficulty. The effective score is the larger of the original score and the resit score capped at half that component's maximum. Preserve both records. A valid passing sitting cannot be repeated to improve the mark. Approved technical-fault or accommodation equivalents replace invalid sittings without consuming the resit. Projects and portfolio samples use the published feedback/revision window.

Model access depends on safety admission and the approved model route, never on exam scores. Real model calls use one approved hosted API configuration with a domestic and an overseas option; you choose one, and comparisons such as P3 stay within that option. You use your own API key and pay for your own usage. The key stays on your machine and only the launcher reads it. Each run has request and token limits, and a run that reaches its limit stops. We recommend setting a spending limit with your provider.

If you cannot or prefer not to pay, cannot register in your region, or do not want to give personal data to a commercial provider, you may choose the replay route without giving a reason. You replay teacher-verified recordings of real runs through course_llm, still predict, check, recompute and explain the result yourself, and label the work as replay. You still declare one option in P1 and replay that option's recordings. The mark ceiling is the same, and choosing replay is never a negative factor in any mark. M3 is unaffected: the teaching team runs every student's model audit (see the project section). Never share, borrow or pay for another student's key.

After you build the small controller, you drive one mature coding agent on ci-dashboard inside the course launcher sandbox. The tool has no general network access; its only egress is the host-mounted relay, which is part of the launcher's host-side broker, allows only the approved endpoint and adds your key outside the sandbox, so you need no separate tool subscription. On the replay route you work from teacher recordings of the same tool.

Until that relay passes its independent security review, use the course mini-agent and teacher recordings instead.

You complete the project on your own; there are no groups and no contribution adjustment. Your project score is your own 0-20, with the published project rubric, written reasons, appeals and human review. Agree one small change in M0 and carry the same path through M1-M4.

M3 works like this. Tag a frozen snapshot of your repository and submit the tag by the published deadline. The teaching team runs the course model audit on it once with the teaching-team key, keeps the original output and run record, and sends you a copy; you need no key of your own. The audit covers evaluation, security and maintenance. The snapshot must contain only your own work in the course repository: no participant material, credentials or anyone else's private code. For every finding, either confirm it with a test that fails before the fix or a repeatable reproduction, refute it with runnable evidence, or say what is missing and why you cannot decide. Then check one place yourself: add a problem the model missed, or state what you checked and found nothing. Marks depend only on your verification: whether the evidence runs, whether it supports your conclusion and whether you claimed more than you checked. The number or severity of model findings earns nothing, and model output never enters a grader. Confirmed findings carry into M4.

Open-source contributions

You submit two or three pull requests to approved public open-source projects, worth 30 points in two samples:

SampleWeeksWorkPoints
C1: warm-up contribution3-6One small, complete fix (a defect, a test or documentation)8
C2: substantial contribution7-13One approved feature or non-trivial fix, in one or two pull requests22

The contributions rubric gives the criteria and points for C1 and C2.

The teaching team publishes the approved project list before week 3. It includes public open-source projects maintained by partner companies and other active projects. Each listed project has an OSI-approved licence, a contribution guide, maintainers who have responded recently, AI-use rules that allow your planned use, a build and tests that run offline within the launcher's limits, and maintainers who have agreed to receive course contributions. At most five students work on one project at a time. You may propose another project; it is approved on the same conditions. Submit the C2 proposal at the end of week 6; the teacher approves the project, issue and scope before week 7.

Contributions are marked with the same explanation check, verification evidence and AI-use note as other open work, plus your upstream collaboration: issue discussion, revisions after review and following the project's rules. A merge or a maintainer reply is not required. Contribute only to public repositories. Never use code from your residency, employer or a partner company's internal systems, and never send it to a model. If a project bans AI-generated contributions, choose another project or work on it without a model. Model-generated or suggested code runs only through the course launcher; prefetch upstream dependencies at locked versions and hashes before the launcher goes offline, and push to the upstream project from your own machine, outside the sandbox. Contributions without any model, or on the replay route, have the same mark ceiling.

You may use a pseudonymous account where the project allows it; the teaching team keeps the mapping privately. You are never asked to sign a CLA that assigns your copyright to a company, and signing a CLA that only grants a non-exclusive licence is your informed choice. If you cannot or prefer not to publish publicly, or do not want to sign a CLA or DCO, you may take the course-hosted route without giving a reason: you submit C1 and C2 of the same scope to a non-public course practice repository, where a TA reviews them as an upstream maintainer would. The rubric and maximum mark are the same, and the choice is never a negative factor in any mark.

The week 14 demonstration is an expo: everyone demonstrates at the same time while staff and classmates move between stations and ask questions, and each student answers at least one staff question.

Workload and participation

Each week has a published hour estimate that covers Week 0 installation, safety admission and API key setup, classes, waiting, help, exams, project work, user recruitment, feedback and revision. The estimates are planning guidance, not a cap: they are updated when a week's required work changes and recalibrated from timed pilot runs. If you go over an estimate, ask for help first; you are never marked down for exceeding a time box. Week 2 includes a required session dissection of about two and a half hours. Rounded up for the published planning value, current estimates are: Week 0, 3; Weeks 1-14, 9, 12, 16, 14, 14, 13, 12, 12, 12, 12, 13, 11, 12 and 11. Weeks 3-13 include work on the open-source contributions; all estimates will be recalibrated from timed pilot runs. P1/P2/P3 are estimated at 11/3/4 hours including workshop time; the session dissection is part of P1, not a second submission. Project workshops are already part of their milestones. Use one principal work entry per week and reference the same evidence across related assessment items.

In weeks 3, 6, 10 and 13 a practitioner from a partner organization joins the first class for up to 45 minutes. Bring one question, and afterwards write one sentence comparing what you heard with that week's evidence. Guest talks are not assessed.

Run two discovery activities of your own for M0. Complete three usability sessions of your own across weeks 11-13. Obtain informed consent before participation, collect minimal data and honor withdrawal; use an actually approved equivalent activity when participation is unavailable. Text rehearsal is not a participant session. Keyboard and approved accessible participation preserve the same learning outcomes and attainable marks.

AI use and responsibility

Use approved assistance for open work. Complete checkpoints, oral answers, initial personal review, discovery notes and personal reflection independently. Attach AI use once to the artifact and reuse its ID; submit only task-relevant traces, not unrelated chat history. Never include private records or credentials in prompts or submissions. Every real model call goes to an approved external provider. Participant material, including raw interview and test material and anything derived from it, is never sent to a model, whatever the consent. Participant material means the notes, records, quotations, observations and media produced in interviews and tests, and their de-identified versions. Requirements, acceptance criteria, tests and hypotheses you write yourself are not participant material if they neither quote a participant nor describe what a particular participant said or did, and you may use them with the approved model route; citing only an activity ID such as [interview:…] does not change this. Never send private code or personal data to a model without proper authorization. Keep your API key out of prompts, the repository, submissions, traces, screenshots and logs.

Explain what changed, why, which inputs are most likely to fail and what you verified yourself. Grading uses observable work and explanation; writing style is not an AI-authorship verdict. Evidence conflicts require a human process and an opportunity to respond.
